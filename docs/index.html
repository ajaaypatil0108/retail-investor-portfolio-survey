Summary

This study asks how retail investors choose and manage their investments. It combines a review of portfolio-management principles with a questionnaire answered by 30 people in Ulhasnagar, a business centre in Thane district, Maharashtra.

Key findings

  1. Mutual funds lead, followed by gold and silver. 66.7% would include mutual funds. Gold and silver (53.3%) and real estate (50%) rank above direct shares (46.7%).
  2. Liquidity matters, but so does property. 86.7% want easy liquidity, yet half would include real estate, which is slow to sell. Most respondents (83.3%) also accept moderate or high risk.
  3. Some expect very high returns. One in three expects returns of 50% or 100%. The time period was not recorded; as yearly figures, these would be far above what a diversified portfolio usually earns.
  4. Monitoring is frequent. 56.7% review and rebalance monthly and 36.7% buy or sell monthly. Long term is the most common horizon (43.3%), though not a majority.
  5. Commitment is strong. 86.7% are likely to continue investing in their portfolio.

1Introduction

Portfolio management is the process of choosing and managing a group of investments, such as shares, bonds, mutual funds, gold and property, so that they earn the best return for the level of risk an investor will accept. The aim is a diversified portfolio that fits the investor's goals, time horizon and risk tolerance. Spreading money across securities and asset classes lowers total risk, because a fall in one holding can be offset by others.

Portfolio managers work through three main decisions:

  • Asset allocation: how to divide the portfolio across asset classes, given the investor's risk tolerance and objectives.
  • Security selection: which securities to hold within each asset class.
  • Market timing: adjusting holdings in response to economic and market developments.

Investors can manage their own portfolios or hire professionals at banks, financial institutions and investment firms. This study focuses on retail investors: individuals who invest their own money, usually in smaller amounts than institutions and with fewer resources. Retail investors often rely on their own research, which can leave them more exposed to emotional biases and short-term news. No plan removes risk entirely, so the goal is to manage it.

The study uses investment in its financial sense: putting money into assets such as shares, bonds, deposits or property to earn income or capital growth. This differs from economic investment, which means adding to a country's productive capital, such as factories, machinery and workforce skills.

2Investment avenues in India

Indian investors can choose between financial assets, such as shares, mutual funds and government securities, and real assets, such as gold and property. Each avenue offers a different mix of return, safety and liquidity.

AvenueWhat it is
SharesOwnership stakes in a company, traded on stock exchanges. Prices move with company performance, market conditions and investor sentiment.
Debentures and bondsDebt issued by companies and governments that pays interest and returns the principal at maturity.
Public depositsFixed-term, fixed-rate deposits placed with companies or non-banking financial companies. They are unsecured, so they carry default risk.
Bank depositsSavings, fixed and recurring deposits. Easy to access, regulated and covered by deposit insurance up to a limit.
Post office savingsGovernment-backed savings schemes such as savings accounts, recurring deposits and time deposits, widely used in rural areas.
Public Provident Fund (PPF)A government-backed 15-year savings scheme with tax benefits under Section 80C, a government-set interest rate, and partial withdrawals and loans after set periods.
Money market instrumentsShort-term debt maturing within a year, such as treasury bills, commercial paper and certificates of deposit. Low risk and highly liquid.
Mutual fundsPool money from many investors into shares, bonds and other securities, offering diversification, liquidity and professional management for a fee.
Life insurance schemesFinancial protection for dependants through term or whole-life policies. Some policies build cash value, and premiums can carry tax benefits.
Real estateResidential, commercial or industrial property. Returns come from rent and price appreciation.
Gold and silverPrecious metals held as a store of value and a hedge against inflation and economic uncertainty, through coins, bars, jewellery or exchange-traded funds.
DerivativesContracts such as futures and options whose value comes from an underlying asset, used to hedge risk or to speculate.
CommoditiesRaw materials such as metals, crude oil, natural gas, wheat, cotton and sugar, traded on the spot market or for future delivery.

3Approaches to managing a portfolio

ApproachHow it worksTrade-off
ActiveThe manager researches and picks securities, trading often to beat the market.Aims for above-market returns, at higher cost and risk.
PassiveHolds the securities of a benchmark index, such as the Nifty 50 or the S&P 500.Low cost and low turnover; returns track the index.
StrategicSets a long-term target allocation from the investor's goals and risk tolerance, and rebalances back to it.Disciplined and stable, but slow to react.
TacticalMoves away from the target allocation for short periods to take advantage of market opportunities.Possible extra return, with more volatility.
DynamicAdjusts the portfolio frequently, often using quantitative models.Used by hedge funds and experienced investors; the highest turnover.

Whichever approach is used, portfolio management matters for four reasons:

  • Diversification: spreading money across assets limits the damage any single holding can do.
  • Risk management: weighing the risk and return of each asset against the investor's goals and tolerance.
  • Returns: regular monitoring and adjustment help the portfolio earn more over time.
  • Tax efficiency: techniques such as tax-loss harvesting and holding assets in the right type of account reduce the tax bill.

4Research design

Objectives

  • Understand how retail investors in Ulhasnagar choose and manage their investments.
  • Identify the assets they prefer, their risk appetite, return expectations and investment horizon, and how often they review and trade.
  • Compare these habits with standard portfolio-management principles and suggest improvements.

Data

  • Primary data: a questionnaire answered by 30 people in Ulhasnagar, Thane district, Maharashtra. The report records results for twelve questions, covering age, gender, whether they invest, horizon, preferred investments, income received, expected return, risk preference, review frequency, liquidity, trading frequency and whether they plan to continue.
  • Secondary data: books, websites, newspapers and business magazines.

Analysis

Each question is summarised as the share of all 30 respondents choosing each answer, with the number of respondents in brackets. Chart titles summarise each question rather than quote its exact wording. Ulhasnagar was chosen because it is an active trading and business centre.

5Review of literature

The study reviewed four widely read books on investing.

The Intelligent Investor — Benjamin Graham (1949)
Graham, a Columbia professor widely seen as the father of value investing, separates defensive from enterprising investors and argues for buying with a margin of safety. The book stresses discipline: decisions should rest on value, not on market mood.
A Random Walk Down Wall Street — Burton Malkiel (1973)
Malkiel, an economist at Princeton, argues that short-term price movements are largely unpredictable, so few investors beat the market consistently. He makes the case for low-cost index funds, the idea behind the passive approach in Section 3.
The Four Pillars of Investing — William Bernstein (2002)
Bernstein, a neurologist turned financial theorist, builds his guide on four pillars: the theory, history, psychology and business of investing. He stresses asset allocation and the role of investors' own behaviour.
Investment Valuation — Aswath Damodaran (1996)
Damodaran, a finance professor at NYU Stern, explains how to value any asset from its expected cash flows, growth and risk. It covers the tools used in security selection.

6Survey findings

Who responded

The sample is young: 83.3% of respondents are aged 18–25. The gender split is close to even, at 53.3% male and 46.7% female.

Investing status and horizon

Four in five respondents currently invest. Long term is the most common horizon (43.3%), but short-term investors are close behind (36.7%).

What they would hold

Mutual funds are the clear favourite. Gold and silver come second, ahead of direct shares, and half the respondents would hold real estate. Money market instruments, one of the most liquid options on the list, are among the least chosen.

Returns and risk

Most respondents accept risk: 43.3% prefer moderate risk and 40% prefer high risk, while only 16.7% prefer low risk. Expectations vary widely. A third expect 10% and another third 20%, but 20% expect 50% and 13.3% expect 100%. Most respondents (76.7%) have already received income from their investments.

How they manage their portfolios

Monitoring is frequent. 56.7% review and rebalance every month and 36.7% buy or sell every month. Only 20% trade just when needed. Nearly all respondents (86.7%) want easy liquidity, and the same share plan to continue investing.

7Discussion

The top choice suits small investors. Mutual funds, chosen by two in three respondents, give small investors diversification and professional management in one product. As a group, respondents also spread their interest across gold, property, shares and deposits.

Liquidity needs and asset choices pull in different directions. Almost every respondent wants easy liquidity, yet half would hold real estate and over a third would hold life insurance schemes, both hard to turn into cash quickly. Investors who want quick access to their money need to keep enough of it in liquid assets.

Some return expectations look high. A third of respondents expect returns of 50% or 100%. The survey did not record over what period, so these may not be yearly targets. If they are, they are well above what a diversified portfolio typically earns, and expectations that high can lead investors to take on more risk than they intend, or to give up when results fall short.

Frequent trading has costs. Monthly review is sensible, but monthly buying and selling adds transaction costs and taxes and invites reactions to short-term news. Malkiel and Bernstein both favour a set allocation that is rebalanced on a schedule.

Because the survey kept only totals for each question, these patterns describe the sample as a whole. They do not show whether, for example, the same people who want high returns also trade monthly.

8Conclusions and suggestions

Portfolio management means choosing, monitoring and adjusting a mix of assets to earn the best return for an acceptable level of risk. Its core tools are asset allocation across asset classes, risk management covering market, credit, liquidity and inflation risk, and regular review. Most respondents in this study invest (80%) and plan to continue (86.7%), and mutual funds are their top choice. Their trading frequency and some of their return expectations leave room for improvement.

Suggestions

  1. Set objectives first. Define goals, time horizon and risk tolerance, then choose an asset allocation to match.
  2. Keep return expectations realistic. Base targets on what each asset class has delivered over time, not on recent winners.
  3. Review often, trade less. Monthly review is fine; rebalance on a fixed schedule or when the allocation drifts, rather than trading monthly.
  4. Match liquidity to needs. Keep emergency money and near-term needs in liquid assets before committing to property or long lock-in products.
  5. Stay diversified at low cost. Index funds and ETFs keep costs down, and investing a fixed amount each month through a systematic investment plan (SIP) smooths out market swings.
  6. Consider taxes. Factor in the tax treatment of each investment, and use tax-advantaged options such as PPF where they fit.
  7. Stay disciplined. Stick to the plan, take a long-term view and avoid following the crowd or reacting to short-term news.
  8. Seek advice when needed. A qualified financial adviser can help with complex decisions.

9Limitations

  • Small sample. With 30 respondents, each person moves a result by 3.3 percentage points.
  • One city. All respondents are from Ulhasnagar, so results may not hold elsewhere.
  • Young sample. 83.3% of respondents are aged 18–25, so the findings mainly describe young investors.
  • Preferences, not holdings. Every single-choice question has 30 answers, so the six respondents who do not currently invest answered them too. The asset, risk and return questions therefore record preferences rather than actual portfolios.
  • Totals only. The individual responses are no longer available, so the analysis is descriptive and cannot compare groups, such as younger and older investors.
  • No time period for returns. Expected returns were recorded without a stated time period.
